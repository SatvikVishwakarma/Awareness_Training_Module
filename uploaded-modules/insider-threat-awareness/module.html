🏢

Not every threat comes from outside the organization. Insider threats — whether malicious, negligent, or compromised — are among the most damaging and hardest to detect.

This training will help you identify the signs, understand the types of insiders, and know exactly when and how to report suspicious behavior.

⚠️ The Three Types of Insider Threats

Understanding why insiders act — intentional, accidental, or manipulated — is key to recognizing the warning signs.

😈 Malicious Insider

An authorized user who deliberately misuses their access for personal gain, revenge, or to aid an external party.

Disgruntled employee Termination pending Financial pressure Recruited spy
Example: A departing engineer downloads the full customer database the week before their last day.

🤦 Negligent Employee

An employee who bypasses security policies or makes careless mistakes — not intending harm, but causing it anyway.

Phishing victim Policy shortcuts Weak passwords Lost devices
Example: An employee emails a sensitive report to their personal Gmail "just to finish it at home."

🔓 Compromised Account

A legitimate user's credentials have been stolen or hijacked. The account is active — but the attacker is in control.

Credential stuffing Phishing Social engineering Malware
Example: A manager's account suddenly accesses file shares it has never touched before, at 2 AM from a foreign IP.

🔎 Data Theft Indicators

Insider data theft rarely happens in a single action. Watch for patterns of behavior — any single indicator may be innocent, but combinations raise red flags.

📤
Unusual volume of data transfers Large file downloads, uploads to personal cloud storage (Dropbox, Google Drive), or emailing files externally — especially outside business hours.
🕐
After-hours access Logging in at unusual hours (late nights, weekends) or from unusual locations, particularly if not consistent with job requirements.
🗂️
Accessing files outside job scope A marketing employee accessing HR salary data, or a developer querying customer PII tables they've never touched before.
🖨️
Excessive printing or copying Printing sensitive documents in bulk, bringing USB drives to work, or taking screenshots of restricted screens.
😤
Behavioral changes before departure Employees who gave notice, were passed over for promotions, or are under performance review accessing or downloading more data than usual.
🔑
Credential sharing or privilege abuse Sharing login credentials with others, requesting unnecessary access rights, or using a colleague's account to access restricted areas.

📢 Reporting Suspicious Behavior

One of the most effective defenses against insider threats is a culture where employees feel safe and empowered to speak up. When in doubt, report it.

⚖️ The Bystander Dilemma

Many employees witness suspicious behavior but don't report it — fearing they're wrong, it's not their place, or they'll harm a colleague. Remember: you're not accusing anyone of a crime. You're providing information that lets trained professionals assess the situation.

1
Document what you observed Note the who, what, when, and where. Screenshots, timestamps, and specific details are more valuable than vague impressions.
2
Do not confront the individual Confronting a potential insider can trigger data destruction, retaliation, or flight. Leave investigation to security professionals.
3
Report through official channels Use your company's security hotline, anonymous tip line, or report directly to your security team or HR. Use established processes, not informal Slack messages.
4
Protect confidentiality Don't discuss what you saw with coworkers. This could tip off the subject, contaminate the investigation, or create legal exposure for you.
5
Cooperate with the investigation Be prepared to provide additional information to investigators. Reporters are protected from retaliation under most corporate policies and relevant laws.
